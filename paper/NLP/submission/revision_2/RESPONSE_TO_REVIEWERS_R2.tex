\concern{Reviewer \#2, Concern \#1}

\concern{Reviewer comment:}

\rvcomment{1. Please formalize the ``near-boundary'' component of the operational review rule in
Section 4 and Algorithm 1. The function $g$ maps a letter rating to a decision class, such as
IG/HY, and $\hat r$ is subsequently defined as the modal decision class. It is therefore unclear how
the condition $\hat r \in B$ identifies a rating ``near'' the IG/HY boundary.}

\textbf{Author response:}

\begin{itemize}
\item We agree. The original rule incorrectly defined boundary proximity using the modal IG/HY class,
which cannot encode distance from the BBB$-$/BB$+$ boundary. We now define proximity using the
modal letter rating and its notch distance from that boundary. Because instability also
occurs away from the boundary, proximity is used as a priority indicator rather than a mandatory
referral condition.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Author action:}

\begin{itemize}
\item Algorithm 1 and Section 4 now define near-boundary status from the modal letter rating
using the BBB$-$/BB$+$ midpoint, while referral is based separately on the dissent count
$d \ge c_{\mathrm{ref}}$.
\item Section 4 removes the earlier claim that boundary proximity reproduces the WATCH
stratum and removes the outdated ``roughly halves to about 33\%'' statement.
\item Section 4 now reports that requiring both disagreement and near-boundary status would
cover 13.3\% of profiles with recall 0.176; referring on any disagreement covers 88.9\%.
\end{itemize}

\concern{Reviewer \#2, Concern \#2}

\concern{Reviewer comment:}

\rvcomment{2. Please clarify the sampling convention underlying the reported matched-pair flip
probabilities. The Methods and the captions of Figures 2--4 should state whether two draws may
select the same elicitation cell and whether the pair may differ in specification, seed, or both.
This clarification would make the 25.2\% result and the self-consistency analysis fully
reproducible.}

\textbf{Author response:}

\begin{itemize}
\item We agree that the sampling convention required explicit definition. The reported 25.2\% flip
probability is the probability that two independent draws with replacement from the same
profile's empirical elicitation distribution fall in different classes. The profile is fixed; the
draws may differ in specification, seed, or both, and identical-cell draws count as agreement.
\item Using sampling without replacement changes the pooled estimate only from 25.2\% to 25.45\%. We
therefore retain the original measure but now label it explicitly as total elicitation
instability, rather than attributing it solely to specification variation.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Author action:}

\begin{itemize}
\item Section 6.3 and the Figure 2 caption now specify replacement, identical-cell treatment,
permitted sources of variation, and the fixed-profile condition.
\item Section 3.5 now labels the 25.2\% estimate as total elicitation disagreement and
separately reports 24.3\% after collapsing seeds within specification and 6.9\% for seed variation
within an identical profile$\times$specification cell.
\item Figure 4 caption now states the actual binomial voting procedure used; the
pair-sampling description was removed from Figure 3 because Fleiss $\kappa$ does not use sampled
pairs.
\end{itemize}

\concern{Reviewer \#2, Concern \#4}

\concern{Reviewer comment:}

\rvcomment{4. In Table 4 and Section 3.6, the statement that approximately 33\% agreement is
``expected by chance'' requires qualification. If this is a uniform three-class reference value,
please label it as such. If it is intended as a chance-corrected baseline based on the observed
class marginals, the corresponding calculation should be provided.}

\textbf{Author response:}

\begin{itemize}
\item We agree. The earlier 33.3\% value was only a uniform three-class reference and was not the

\item Accordingly, this arm does not show agreement above the marginal-matching chance benchmark.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Author action:}

\begin{itemize}
\item Section 3.6 and Table 4 now report observed agreement, marginal-matching expected
agreement, excess agreement with its confidence interval, and Cohen's $\kappa$.
\item Section 3.6 no longer interprets machine--agency agreement as evidence of rating
accuracy.
\end{itemize}

\concern{Reviewer \#3, Concern \#1}

\concern{Reviewer comment:}

\rvcomment{the authors have made efforts to address the previous review comments, including
reframing the contribution as an evaluation-methodology case study, removing the economic
translations, correcting the permutation test, adding a reasoning-architecture arm, and providing
held-out-specification validation for the SIS\dots\ however, the revised manuscript still requires
careful evaluation, and the following points must be considered before it can be further considered
for publication. this manuscript is a research article, and it is evaluated as such.}

\rvcomment{the per-axis variance decomposition remains non-identified under the
resolution-limited d-optimal fractional grid, yet the abstract, highlights, and conclusions still
state that no controllable axis exceeds about 5\% of rating variance. the authors now report
aliasing diagnostics, including a maximum absolute correlation of 0.333 between a main effect and a
two-factor interaction of other factors, and they correctly label the decomposition as descriptive.
however, the headline claim of ``no axis exceeds \textasciitilde5\%'' is still presented as a
substantive finding rather than as a model-dependent descriptive partition. because main effects can
be partially aliased with omitted interactions, the per-axis point estimates cannot support even a
descriptive ranking of axes without explicit bounds or a design that separates them. the added
R\textsuperscript{2} range of 0.083 to 0.119 across model specifications does not identify individual
axes; it only shows that the elicitation axes jointly explain little variance under the assumed
models. this remains important because the paper's central interpretation---that instability is
spread across elicitation choices rather than concentrated in a tunable knob---depends in part on
this decomposition.}

\rvcomment{required action: either remove all per-axis numeric claims from the abstract, highlights,
and conclusions, or conduct a fully crossed or resolution-v sub-experiment on a reduced set of
factors and items that can cleanly estimate main effects and relevant two-way interactions. if a new
experiment is not feasible, provide formal bounds on each per-axis variance share under all aliasing
structures consistent with the observed design matrix, or fit a bayesian hierarchical model with
explicit shrinkage priors on interaction terms and report posterior intervals for the main effects.
the abstract and highlights should then state only what is identified: that the elicitation axes
jointly explain a small and model-dependent share of variance, not that any specific axis is bounded
by \textasciitilde5\%.}

\textbf{Author response:}

\begin{itemize}
\item We agree that the fractional design cannot identify individual elicitation-axis variance shares
when omitted two-way interactions are allowed. We therefore removed the per-axis interpretation
rather than attempting to bound or rank individual axes.
\item Formally, omitted interactions imply
\[
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}] = \beta + A\gamma,
\]
and the design places no finite restriction on $\lVert\gamma\rVert_2$; therefore no finite
design-only bound exists for an individual main effect. Recomputed diagnostics on the actual
32-specification design confirm substantial aliasing: the maximum main-effect--interaction
correlation is 0.504, the largest canonical correlation is 1.000, and six of eight exceed 0.9.
\item The retained result is only that the elicitation axes jointly explain
$R^2=0.083$--$0.119$ under the fitted models. The model-free disagreement results are
unaffected.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Author action:}

\begin{itemize}
\item Section 6.2 now states explicitly that individual elicitation-axis effects are not
identified and reports the aliasing algebra, corrected design diagnostics, and
canonical-correlation analysis.
\item Abstract, Highlights, Results, and Discussion no longer state that no individual axis
exceeds approximately 5\%.
\item Table 1 no longer reports individual-axis estimates as interpretable variance
shares.
\end{itemize}

\concern{Reviewer \#3, Concern \#2}

\concern{Reviewer comment:}

\rvcomment{the real-issuer arm still does not provide strong external validity, and the
decontamination procedure remains insufficiently validated for the claims made\dots\ the perturbation
applies a single per-issuer uniform scaling factor to all dollar quantities. this preserves financial
ratios and Altman Z'' by construction, but it does not prevent an LLM from inferring issuer identity
from the pattern of ratios, nor does it rule out memorized issuer knowledge that is not based on
exact figures. the fingerprinting test only measures exact-figure lookup. the authors acknowledge
this limitation, but the abstract still states that specification variation is observed in ``31
perturbed and fingerprint-screened real issuers,'' which can be read as external validation. in
addition, the scale factor is confounded with issuer identity because each issuer receives only one
draw. the sample of 31 issuers and 12 specifications is also small for any quantitative external
claim.}

\rvcomment{required action: add a ratio-pattern identification test. for example, train a classifier
on financial ratios to predict issuer identity from the perturbed profiles, or prompt the LLM to
infer the issuer from the ratio profile after perturbation, and report the identification rate.
conduct a within-issuer scale sensitivity design by re-eliciting a subset of issuers at multiple
scale factors, such as 0.6, 1.0, and 1.7, so that scale is not fully confounded with issuer
identity. if these checks show residual identification or if the sample remains too small, remove
the real-issuer claim from the abstract and conclusion and present the arm only as an illustrative
descriptive limitation. the paper should not imply external validity beyond what the design can
support.}

\textbf{Author response:}

\begin{itemize}
\item We agree that uniform scaling alone cannot establish decontamination because it preserves ratio
patterns and originally confounded scale with issuer identity. We therefore performed both
requested checks.
\item First, all 31 issuers were re-elicited at scales 0.6, 1.0, and 1.7 using the frozen
12-specification grid. Extreme-scale classifications differed in 15.9\% of cells versus 15.2\%
under the within-issuer permutation null ($p=0.644$), providing no detectable incremental scale
effect.
\item Second, we tested issuer identification from ratio patterns. The open-ended probe produced no
correct identifications, but the closed-set probe identified the correct issuer in 19.4\% of
substantive calls versus a 3.2\% chance benchmark ($p=5\times10^{-18}$). We therefore do
not treat ratio-preserving scaling as decontamination and retain the real-issuer arm only
as an illustrative descriptive check.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Author action:}

\begin{itemize}
\item Section 3.7 and Table 5 now report the within-issuer scale experiment and ratio-pattern
identification experiment.
\item Abstract, Section 3.6, and Conclusion now describe the real-issuer arm as illustrative
and descriptive and make no external-validity claim from it.
\item Supplementary archive now includes the pre-registered experimental specification,
frozen outputs, manifests, and analysis code.
\end{itemize}

\concern{Reviewer \#3, Concern \#3}

\concern{Reviewer comment:}

\rvcomment{the SIS validation is improved, but its incremental value over simple
disagreement-based baselines and its status as a deployable model-risk control remain unproven\dots\
at the binary IG/HY gate with a low threshold, the SIS flag reduces to ``any disagreement'' across
the k elicitations. the reported Youden-optimal threshold of 0.08 is below the smallest non-zero SIS
value at k=16, so the operating rule is essentially ``flag any issuer with at least one disagreeing
calibration elicitation.'' this rule is already directly observable from the k elicitations and does
not require the continuous SIS. the manuscript does not compare SIS against simple baselines such as
the number of dissenting elicitations, the modal rating's distance to the IG/HY boundary,
self-consistency vote margins, semantic entropy, or token-level confidence. the threshold is also
chosen and evaluated on the same held-out split, and cross-model transfer is moderate and
inconsistent. this matters because the abstract and highlights present the SIS as a ``validated'' and
``deployable model-risk control.''}

\rvcomment{required action: benchmark SIS against these simple baselines in both
held-out-specification and held-out-issuer settings. report a decision-curve analysis or net-benefit
analysis for triage, showing whether SIS improves referral decisions over ``flag any disagreement,''
over distance to the IG/HY boundary, and over self-consistency vote margins. if SIS does not provide
incremental value beyond these baselines, reframe it as a descriptive dispersion index rather than a
deployable control, and remove or substantially qualify the ``validated'' and ``deployable'' language
in the abstract and highlights. if it does provide incremental value, report the threshold selection
in a fully nested cross-validation or on an independent calibration set and show how performance
varies with k and tau.}

\textbf{Author response:}

\begin{itemize}
\item We agree that $\mathrm{SIS}$ must be compared directly with simpler disagreement measures. In
both the held-out-specification analysis and the profile-split subsampling analysis,
$\mathrm{SIS}$ provides no incremental ranking information over dissent count at the
binary IG/HY gate. The previously reported Youden threshold $\tau=0.08$ corresponds to
$d\ge3$ at the retrospective $k=48$ and collapses to $d\ge1$ at $k=16$ (smallest non-zero index
values $0.0408$ and $0.117$). It is no longer used, and the manuscript states the rule directly
on $d$.
\item At that resolution,
\[
\mathrm{SIS}=2(m/k)(1-m/k),
\]
so $\mathrm{SIS}$, dissent count, and vote margin are rank-equivalent. Empirically, each gives AUC
0.936, Spearman correlation 1.000, and identical net-benefit curves. At three bands the measures
are not exactly rank-identical, but they produce the same AUC in the present analyses.
\item We therefore no longer present $\mathrm{SIS}$ as a superior predictive instrument or use a tuned
$\mathrm{SIS}$ threshold operationally. Referral is stated directly in terms of dissent count
$d \ge c_{\mathrm{ref}}$, while $\mathrm{SIS}$ is retained as a descriptive dispersion and
governance representation. Boundary distance performs materially worse; discrete semantic entropy
is again rank-equivalent at the binary gate.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Author action:}

\begin{itemize}
\item Section 4.3 and Table 8 now benchmark $\mathrm{SIS}$ against dissent count, vote
margin, boundary distance, and discrete semantic entropy in both the held-out-specification and
profile-split analyses.
\item Table 9 now reports net-benefit comparisons of the referral strategies against
refer-all and refer-none.
\item Sections 4.1--4.3 now present $\mathrm{SIS}$ as a descriptive dispersion index and
governance representation, with the operational rule stated directly on dissent count.
\item Abstract and Highlights no longer describe $\mathrm{SIS}$ as a validated or deployable
model-risk control.
\end{itemize}

\concern{Reviewer \#3, Concern \#4}

\concern{Reviewer comment:}

\rvcomment{under the resolution-limited d-optimal fractional grid, can you provide formal
identifiable bounds on the maximum possible main effect of answer presentation and the
provider-by-item interaction under all aliasing structures consistent with the observed design
matrix, and how would those bounds change the abstract's claim that no controllable axis exceeds
about 5\% of rating variance?}

\textbf{Author response:}

\begin{itemize}
\item The two quantities have different identification properties.
\item For answer presentation, no finite design-only bound exists because omitted interaction effects
enter the estimated main effect through
\[
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}] = \beta + A\gamma,
\]
and the design provides no finite restriction on $\lVert\gamma\rVert_2$. The former approximately
4.6\% estimate therefore cannot support an identified upper bound or ranking.
\item The provider$\times$item component is different because items are crossed with specifications.
All 1{,}440 item$\times$specification cells are represented; only four are missing one seed, and
the largest realized correlation with a specification-axis contrast is 0.0022. We therefore retain
the approximately 1.4\% provider$\times$item component, subject to this negligible imbalance.
\item The manuscript now reports only the identified joint result: the elicitation axes explain
$R^2=0.083$--$0.119$ under the fitted specifications.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Author action:}

\begin{itemize}
\item Section 6.2 now provides the formal aliasing analysis and numerical diagnostics
establishing why no finite design-only bound exists for individual elicitation-axis effects.
\item Abstract and Highlights no longer contain the approximately 5\% per-axis claim.
\item Table 1 no longer reports individual-axis estimates as interpretable variance
shares.
\end{itemize}
